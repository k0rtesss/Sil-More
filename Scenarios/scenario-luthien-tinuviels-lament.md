# Tinuviel's Lament: The Song Before Mandos

## Scenario Design Document

---

## I. Lore Foundation

### The Song of Luthien Before Mandos (Silmarillion, Chapter 19)

When Beren Erchamion fell at the Hunting of the Wolf, slain by the venom of
Carcharoth, Luthien Tinuviel's spirit departed from her body out of grief and
came to the Halls of Mandos. There she sang before the Doomsman a song of such
surpassing beauty and sorrow that Mandos himself was moved to pity -- the only
time in all the history of Arda that this befell.

Tolkien writes: "The song of Luthien before Mandos was the song most fair that
ever in words was woven, and the song most sorrowful that ever the world shall
hear. Unchanged, imperishable, it is sung still in Valinor beyond the hearing
of the world, and listening the Valar are grieved."

She wove two themes: the sorrow of the Eldar, bound to the circles of a world
that grows old and dims, and the grief of Men, who pass beyond the world into
a fate unknown even to the Valar. The two themes she interlaced, as no voice
before or since has done, and the Halls that hear all sorrows trembled.

Mandos was moved to pity. He went to Manwe and sought counsel. What passed
between them, and what word came from Iluvatar, is not told. But Luthien was
offered a choice: she might go to Valimar and dwell among the Valar in bliss,
relinquishing all memory of grief -- but Beren would not be there. Or she might
return to Middle-earth, and Beren with her, but as mortals both -- Luthien
would lose her immortality, and both would die a true death, and pass beyond
the circles of the world into the fate of Men.

She chose mortality. She chose Beren. And together they returned to
Middle-earth, and dwelt upon Tol Galen in Ossiriand, and bore a son named
Dior, and for a brief time knew peace. Then they died, and their spirits
departed the world, and none know where they went, for the fate of Men is
hidden even from the Valar.

This is the only tale in all the legendarium in which death is defeated.

### Thematic Analysis

Where the Glorfindel scenario tells a story of *remembering forward* -- a hero
recovering lost identity -- and the Feanor scenario tells a story of
*remembering backward* -- a dying spirit reliving its life -- and the Fingolfin
scenario tells a story of *remembering in combat* -- and the Maglor scenario
tells a story of *remembering through confusion* -- and the Sauron scenario
tells a story of *choosing in the present* -- the Luthien scenario tells a
story of **singing into being**.

Luthien does not remember. She does not choose. She *creates*. Her song before
Mandos is not a plea but a *demonstration*. She sings the story of her love
with such power that the story becomes real -- the memories of the people she
loved take shape, walk into the darkness, and bring back light. The Lament is
not passive grief. It is the most active thing in the world: love made audible,
made tangible, made capable of reaching into the deepest dark and returning
with proof that the dark is not all there is.

This is Luthien's nature. She is half-Maia, daughter of Melian the divine and
Thingol the ancient. Her power is not the power of sword or craft but the power
of *song* -- the original power, the power that made the world in the
Ainulindale. When she sang before Morgoth, the mightiest of the Valar fell
asleep. When she sang before Sauron, his fortress crumbled. When she sings
before Mandos, Doom itself bends.

But the scenario's brilliance lies in who she sends into the darkness. Not
kings. Not legends. Not Fingolfin with his silver mail or Feanor with his
burning eyes. She sends *the people she loved*: the hunter who guarded the
border. The minstrel who played in the halls. The captain who never sought
glory. The hound who chose loyalty over his master. The mortal man who walked
into an enchanted forest and fell in love with a song.

These are ordinary heroes. Their power is small. Their names are not written in
the great tales (or if written, only in passing). But Luthien's song makes them
luminous. In the darkness of Angband, an ordinary hunter carrying the love of
Tinuviel's voice is brighter than any king carrying only his own pride.

The tragedy -- and the hope -- is that these fragile, ordinary heroes must do
what the great ones could not: recover light from the darkness. Not because
they are mightier, but because they are *loved*. And love, in Tolkien's world,
is the one power Morgoth cannot corrupt, cannot understand, and cannot defeat.

In gameplay terms, this creates a **fast, punishing campaign** with a
Silmaril-gated victory condition, no death limit, weak heroes, and heavy curse
stacking. The player can try endlessly -- Luthien's song never stops -- but
each failure leaves deeper scars. The question is not *whether* you can win,
but *at what cost*. And the cost determines the quality of the mercy Mandos
grants.

### Key Lore Touchstones Referenced

- **Beren and Luthien's meeting** -- Beren, son of Barahir, wandered into
  Doriath and saw Luthien dancing among the hemlocks of Neldoreth in a glade
  lit by stars. He called her Tinuviel, "nightingale," and from that moment
  was lost. (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **Thingol's demand** -- King Thingol, in scorn, set the bride-price for
  Luthien's hand: a Silmaril from Morgoth's Iron Crown. He intended it as a
  death sentence. It became the greatest quest of the First Age. (Silmarillion,
  "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **Finrod's oath and death** -- Finrod Felagund, who had sworn an oath of
  friendship to Barahir (Beren's father), went with Beren to fulfill that
  oath. In Sauron's dungeon on Tol-in-Gaurhoth, Finrod broke his bonds and
  fought a werewolf with bare hands to save Beren, and died. (Silmarillion,
  "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **The ten companions** -- Ten warriors of Nargothrond followed Finrod and
  Beren to Tol-in-Gaurhoth. All were devoured one by one in Sauron's dungeon.
  One among them was named Edrahil, the most faithful. (HoME III, Lay of
  Leithian)
- **Luthien's escape from Hirilorn** -- Thingol imprisoned Luthien in the great
  beech tree Hirilorn to prevent her from following Beren. She wove an
  enchanted cloak from her own hair and escaped. (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and
  Luthien")
- **Huan the Hound of Valinor** -- Originally given to Celegorm by Orome,
  Huan forsook his master to follow Luthien, recognizing her goodness. He
  overthrew Sauron in wolf-form. He was fated to die only by the mightiest
  wolf ever to walk the world. He spoke three times before his death.
  (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **The overthrow of Tol-in-Gaurhoth** -- Luthien and Huan came to Sauron's
  isle. Sauron sent wolves; Huan slew them. Sauron came forth in wolf-form;
  Huan held him by the throat. Luthien commanded him to yield the tower.
  Sauron surrendered and fled as a vampire. Finrod's companions were freed
  (those who still lived). (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **Before the throne of Morgoth** -- Luthien sang before Morgoth in
  Angband, and the power of her song was so great that Morgoth himself, the
  mightiest of all beings in Arda, fell from his throne in slumber. Beren cut
  a Silmaril from the Iron Crown while Morgoth slept. (Silmarillion, "Of Beren
  and Luthien")
- **Carcharoth and the Silmaril** -- The great wolf Carcharoth, whom Morgoth
  had bred and filled with his own malice, guarded the gates of Angband. As
  Beren and Luthien fled with the Silmaril, Carcharoth bit off Beren's hand
  that held the jewel. The Silmaril burned within his belly and drove him mad.
  (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **The Hunting of the Wolf** -- Thingol, Beren, Huan, Beleg, and Mablung
  hunted Carcharoth. The wolf was slain by Huan, who also died of his wounds.
  Beren was mortally wounded. He died in Luthien's arms, and the Silmaril was
  recovered from Carcharoth's belly. (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **The Halls of Mandos** -- Where Elvish spirits dwell after death, awaiting
  judgment or rebirth. Mandos (Namo) is the Doomsman of the Valar, keeper of
  the Houses of the Dead. His spouse Vaire the Weaver weaves all history into
  tapestries that line the halls. (Silmarillion, "Of the Beginning of Days";
  "Valaquenta")
- **Nellas of Doriath** -- An Elf-woman of Doriath who watched over the young
  Turin in the woods, unseen. She testified at his trial before Thingol. A
  minor figure, representative of the common folk of Doriath. (Narn i Hin
  Hurin; Unfinished Tales)
- **Daeron the Minstrel** -- Greatest minstrel of the Sindar, inventor of the
  Cirth (runic script). He loved Luthien and twice betrayed her to Thingol
  when she sought to follow Beren. After Luthien's departure, he wandered into
  the east and was not seen again. (Silmarillion, "Of Beren and Luthien")
- **The Girdle of Melian** -- The enchantment Melian wove around Doriath,
  protecting it from all evil. None could enter against her will. It was the
  greatest protective enchantment in Middle-earth. (Silmarillion, "Of Thingol
  and Melian")

## II. Scenario Overview

**Name:** Tinuviel's Lament
**Subtitle:** The Song Before Mandos
**Type:** Open Campaign (Silmaril-gated, no fixed run count, no death limit)
**Victory Condition:** Recover 7 Silmarils from Angband
**Defeat Condition:** None. The song never stops. Deaths add curses but do not
end the campaign.
**Hero Pool:** 10 heroes (3 new + 7 existing) -- ordinary people, not legends
**Difficulty:** High (weak heroes, heavy curse stacking)

### Narrative Frame

The player is Luthien Tinuviel, standing as a disembodied spirit in the Halls
of Mandos. Her beloved Beren is dead. Her body lies empty in Doriath. She has
walked the roads of the dead and come before the Doomsman to plead for the
impossible: a mortal man returned from death.

Mandos hears her song and is moved -- but not yet moved enough. He sets a
challenge: seven lights recovered from the Enemy's darkness. Seven proofs that
love endures where all else fails. Bring them, and he will hear her plea in
full.

The heroes Luthien sends into Angband are not herself. They are the verses of
her Lament made flesh -- the memories of the people she loved, given shape by
the power of her song. When she sings of Beren, Beren walks into the dark.
When she sings of Beleg the hunter, Beleg stalks through shadow. The song IS
the game. Each run is a verse. Each Silmaril is a proof.

The heroes are deliberately ordinary. They are not High Kings or Feanorian
princes. They are the hunters and minstrels and soldiers of Doriath, the
mortal outlaws of Dorthonion, the faithful companions who followed into
darkness. Their weakness is the point: Luthien's argument before Mandos is not
that love makes the powerful more powerful. It is that love makes the ordinary
*enough*.

### Progression Structure

The campaign is Silmaril-gated, like the Glorfindel scenario, but differs in
three crucial ways:

1. **No death limit.** Deaths do not end the campaign. They add curses (stat
   and skill penalties) to subsequent runs, creating a spiral of increasing
   difficulty, but the campaign continues until 7 Silmarils are recovered.
   This is unique among all scenarios and reflects Luthien's character: she
   never stops. She never gives up. She will sing until Mandos yields or until
   the world ends.

2. **Weak heroes.** The hero pool averages Power 1, compared to Power 2-3 in
   other scenarios. Individual runs are fast (weak heroes die quickly or
   succeed against the odds) and the margin for error is razor-thin.

3. **Heavy curse stacking.** Each death adds more severe curses than in the
   Glorfindel scenario. The cumulative effect means that late-campaign runs
   with many deaths become nearly impossible. The "soft defeat" is practical
   exhaustion: heroes too cursed to reach Morgoth's throne.

**Victory Path (Type 0 chapters):** 8 chapters, triggered by cumulative
Silmaril count across all runs:

| Silmarils | Chapter | Title | Verse Theme |
|-----------|---------|-------|-------------|
| 0 | 0 | The Halls of Waiting | Luthien arrives, Mandos sets the challenge |
| 1 | 1 | Beneath the Hemlock Stars | The meeting: Beren sees Luthien dance |
| 2 | 2 | The Price No Father Should Ask | Thingol's impossible demand |
| 3 | 3 | The Faithful Who Followed | Finrod's oath, the dungeon, the wolves |
| 4 | 4 | The Tower Broken by Song | Luthien and Huan overthrow Sauron |
| 5 | 5 | Before the Iron Crown | Morgoth sleeps, the Silmaril is won |
| 6 | 6 | The Hand and the Wolf | Carcharoth, the Hunting, Beren falls |
| 7 | 7 | Love Stronger Than Death | Mandos yields, the choice is offered |

**Defeat Path (Type 1 chapters):** 15+ chapters, triggered by cumulative death
count, telling the story of the song faltering under the weight of loss but
never ceasing.

### Scoring System

- Each successful escape from Angband contributes to a combined score
- Progressive diminishing returns: best/1 + second/2 + third/4 + fourth/8...
- The total score (not just Silmaril count) determines the quality of the
  final ending
- Deaths reduce score through accumulated curses on future runs

### Curse Economy

- Each death adds heavy curses (stat and skill penalties) to subsequent runs
- Curse stacking is more severe than in the Glorfindel scenario (reflecting
  the difficulty)
- No blessings system (unlike Glorfindel) -- the only "blessing" is Luthien's
  song itself, which carries the heroes forward despite the curses
- The absence of blessings reflects the scenario's theme: there is no external
  aid, no Valar quest rewards, no oath bonuses. Only love. Only the song.

D:Greatest of all hounds, bred in Valinor by Orome the Hunter and given to
D:Celegorm son of Feanor; yet when his master's deeds turned foul, Huan's
D:heart turned truer, and he forsook the hunter for the hunted. He followed
D:Luthien Tinuviel on her quest, not from command but from recognition --
D:the faithful beast perceiving in her a goodness worth any betrayal of
D:allegiance. Upon the isle of werewolves he seized Sauron by the throat in
D:wolf-form and held him until the dark lord yielded the tower. It was
D:foretold that he would die only when he met the mightiest wolf to walk
D:the world; and at the Hunting of the Wolf he slew Carcharoth and was slain,
D:and spoke for the last of his three allotted times: "Farewell, Tinuviel."
```

**Lore notes:** Huan is one of the most beloved figures in the tale of Beren
and Luthien. Tolkien describes him as "holding Sauron by the throat" until
Luthien could command the fortress's surrender. He is explicitly stated to be
from Valinor, of the race of the great hounds bred by Orome. He was "permitted
to speak three times ere his death" -- a gift from the Valar. His first speech
was to counsel Luthien when she was captive by Celegorm. His second was to
plan the assault on Angband. His third and final word was "Farewell" to
Luthien as he died from Carcharoth's venom. Including him as a playable hero
is mechanically unusual (a hound, not an Elf or Man) but narratively
essential: he is the most faithful companion Luthien ever had. His combat
strength is exceptional, but he cannot use standard equipment, songs, or
crafting -- he fights with jaws and courage alone.

```

**Lore notes:** Edrahil is named in the Lay of Leithian (HoME III), the long
narrative poem that Tolkien worked on for years. He is described as the most
loyal of Finrod's warriors, the one who spoke first when the king asked for
volunteers. In the Silmarillion itself, the ten companions are unnamed, but
the detail that they were devoured one by one by wolves in Sauron's dungeon is
preserved. Edrahil represents the archetype of the faithful soldier -- not a
lord, not a legend, just a warrior who followed his king into certain death
because he had sworn an oath and meant it. He is exactly the kind of "ordinary
hero" this scenario celebrates.

Turin in his childhood. Tolkien states that she was "shy, and of the woodlands,
and came never into Menegroth" except when compelled to testify at Turin's
trial. She represents the countless unnamed Elves of Doriath -- the common
folk, the border-dwellers, the ones whose names are not recorded in the great
tales but who lived and loved and endured under the protection of Melian's
Girdle. In a scenario about ordinary heroes, she is the most ordinary of all,
and therefore the most thematically important.

### Design Rationale

The hero pool is deliberately weak. The average Power is 1.2, compared to 2.1
in the Glorfindel scenario and 2.8 in the Feanor scenario. This is the
defining mechanical feature: in a scenario about ordinary heroes, the heroes
must BE ordinary. No hero has Power above 3, and only Finrod reaches that
level (reflecting his canonical status as the noblest of the Noldor).

Every hero represents a facet of Luthien's love and the world she knew:

- **Beren** -- The center. The beloved. Every verse of the Lament is ultimately
  about him: the mortal man who stumbled into an enchanted forest and changed
  the history of the world through sheer stubbornness and love. He is Power 1.
  Not a great warrior. Not a king. Just a man who loved, and was loved, and
  would not stop.

- **Finrod** -- The oath-keeper. The noblest figure in the pool, and the only
  one with Power 3. His presence is the exception that proves the rule: Finrod
  was noble not because of his lineage but because of his *character*. He died
  in a dungeon, wrestling a werewolf, to save a mortal man he barely knew --
  because he had given his word to that man's father. He is legendary, but his
  legend is one of humility and faithfulness.

- **Daeron** -- The other singer. Luthien's mirror and her betrayer. He loved
  her, and his love was not returned, and the rejection drove him to betray
  her twice to Thingol. But his music was second only to hers among the
  Children of Iluvatar, and in the song before Mandos, even Daeron's broken
  love has a place. He represents the truth that love is not always requited,
  not always noble, but is always real.

- **Beleg** -- The guardian. Chief march-warden of Doriath, who patrolled the
  borders of Melian's Girdle and kept the realm safe. He would later die for
  Turin's sake -- another example of faithfulness unto death. Luthien would
  have known him as the silent, tireless protector of her forest home.

- **Mablung** -- The soldier. Of the Heavy Hand. Captain under Thingol, who
  participated in the Hunting of the Wolf alongside Beren and Huan. He is the
  ordinary warrior, the one who does not seek glory but accepts duty. He held
  the line. He was there when it mattered.

- **Celeborn** -- The kinsman. Prince of Doriath, later husband of Galadriel.
  He represents the continuity of Luthien's world -- the realm that endured,
  the people who survived. His presence in the Lament is a verse about home:
  the silver beeches of Doriath, the halls of Menegroth, the life Luthien
  knew before the quest changed everything.

- **Barahir** -- The father Luthien never met. Lord of Ladros, who saved
  Finrod's life in the Dagor Bragollach and received from him the ring of
  Felagund as a pledge of friendship. That ring was the reason Finrod went
  with Beren. That ring was, in a sense, the origin of the entire quest.
  Luthien knows Barahir through Beren's stories, through the ring her beloved
  wore, through the chain of oaths and loyalties that led to love.

- **Huan** -- The companion. Not Elf, not Man, but faithful beyond either
  kindred. He chose Luthien over his master Celegorm because he recognized
  her goodness. He fought beside her. He died for her world. He is the most
  powerful hero in combat (STR 3) but cannot use equipment normally -- he
  fights as a beast, with jaws and loyalty.

- **Edrahil** -- The unnamed brave. One of ten who followed into certain death.
  He represents all the unnamed heroes of the First Age -- the soldiers, the
  companions, the ones whose names are footnotes or whose names are lost. In
  Luthien's song, his name is spoken, and for one moment he stands in the
  light.

- **Nellas** -- The least. Power 0. The weakest hero in any scenario. A
  shy wood-elf who watched over a child from the branches and trembled before
  a king. She is the thematic heart of the scenario: if Luthien's love can
  carry even Nellas into Angband and back with a Silmaril, then love truly
  is stronger than death.

## V. Narrative Script

### Opening Scene (Plays Once, at Campaign Start)

> **Title: The Halls of Waiting**
>
> You are dead.
>
> Or rather -- your body lies far away, in Doriath beneath the falling leaves,
> empty as a house whose light has gone out. Your spirit walked the grey roads
> until it came here, to the Halls of Mandos, where the fates of the Eldar are
> kept and the dead await their doom.
>
> The Halls are vast. Silent. Grey stone rising to vaults beyond sight, lined
> with tapestries that tell the history of Arda from its first note to its
> last -- Vaire's weaving, thread by thread, age by age. In the dim light
> you see your own story woven there: a glade beneath hemlock trees, a mortal
> man with leaves in his hair, a jewel of impossible brightness, a wolf, a
> hand, a farewell.
>
> But you have not come to wait.
>
> You have come to sing.
>
> The song rises from you as naturally as grief, as inevitably as breath. It
> is not a plea. It is not a prayer. It is a *telling* -- the story of Beren
> and Luthien woven into melody, two themes intertwined: the ageless sorrow
> of the Eldar, who are bound to the world and must watch it diminish, and
> the brief bright grief of Men, who pass beyond the world into a fate the
> Valar themselves do not know.
>
> You sing of a mortal man who wandered into an enchanted forest.
> You sing of an Elf-maiden who danced beneath the stars.
> You sing of a love that should not have been, and was.
> You sing of a quest that should have been impossible, and was not.
> You sing of a wolf and a hand and a farewell and a death.
> You sing of love stronger than death.
>
> The Halls tremble. The tapestries stir. The grey air itself seems to listen.
>
> And Mandos speaks.
>
> His voice is older than the world. It is the voice of stone foundations, of
> fate inscribed before the first sunrise, of endings known before beginnings
> are begun. He is the Doomsman. He does not weep. He does not pity. He is
> the record and the reckoning.
>
> *"Thy song is fair, Tinuviel -- fairer than any voice that has entered these
> halls since the music that made the world. I who remember all things do not
> remember being moved. And yet thy voice stirs even stone to remembrance of
> what it was before it hardened."*
>
> He pauses. The silence is immense.
>
> *"But words are air, and air passes. The Doom of the Firstborn and the
> Secondborn was set by Iluvatar ere the world was wrought. I cannot unmake
> what He made. Thy beloved is beyond the circles of this world, gone into
> the fate of Men, and even I know not where that road leads."*
>
> You do not stop singing.
>
> *"Yet... if thy love is as thy song declares -- stronger than shadow,
> brighter than the jewels of Feanor that even now burn in the Enemy's crown
> -- then let it be proved. Not in words. In deeds. Seven lights recovered
> from the deepest dark. Seven proofs that love endures where all else fails.
> Bring them to these halls, and I shall hear thy plea in full, and seek the
> counsel of Manwe, and perhaps... perhaps even the thought of Iluvatar."*
>
> A door opens in the grey stone -- not into death, but into shadow. Through
> it, you see the pits of Angband, the fortress of the Enemy, where the
> stolen light still burns in an iron crown.
>
> You cannot go yourself. You are a spirit, a voice, a song.
>
> But a song can give shape to memory. A voice can call the beloved from
> silence. The people you knew -- the hunter, the hound, the minstrel, the
> captain, the mortal man who loved you -- their faces rise in your Lament,
> and through the power of your singing, they walk into the shadow.
>
> They are not kings. They are not legends.
>
> They are the people you loved. And that is enough.

### Victory Path Chapters (Type 0: Silmaril Recovery)

These chapters trigger as the cumulative Silmaril count across all runs
increases. Each represents a verse of Luthien's Lament reaching completion --
another proof of love laid before the Doomsman.

The seven verses follow the chronology of the Lay of Leithian: the tale of
Beren and Luthien from first meeting to last breath. As Luthien sings each
verse and the proof is brought, Mandos is drawn deeper into the story. His
responses shift from cold formality to something unprecedented: feeling.

---

#### Verse 1 -- Beneath the Hemlock Stars (1 Silmaril)

> A light returns to the grey halls, and the first thread of the Lament
> brightens in Vaire's tapestry.
>
> You sing of the meeting.
>
> Of a mortal man, ragged and road-worn, who stumbled through the Girdle of
> Melian into a land no mortal had entered. Of a glade in the forest of
> Neldoreth where hemlock-umbels grew tall and white, and starlight fell
> through the canopy like silver rain. Of a maiden dancing there, and the
> music was her own, and her feet were bare upon the moss, and her hair was
> dark as the shadow of twilight.
>
> He saw her and was lost. He called out -- not her name, for he did not know
> it -- but a word that came to him unbidden: *Tinuviel*. Nightingale.
>
> And she heard, and paused, and turned, and saw a mortal man standing at the
> edge of the glade with wonder in his eyes and leaves in his hair.
>
> This is the beginning. This is where everything starts: not with oaths or
> swords or Silmarils, but with a man who heard a song and a maiden who heard
> a name.
>
> Mandos listens. One light burns in the grey halls.

---

#### Verse 2 -- The Price No Father Should Ask (2 Silmarils)

> A second light joins the first. The Lament deepens.
>
> You sing of your father's wrath.
>
> Of Thingol Greycloak, King of Doriath, mightiest of the Sindar, who looked
> upon the mortal man his daughter loved and saw only a beggar, a wanderer, an
> insect crawling into his realm uninvited. *"Who are you,"* he said, *"to
> seek the hand of Luthien, who is the fairest thing beneath the sky?"*
>
> And Beren answered: *"I am Beren son of Barahir. I ask for nothing I have
> not earned with my blood."*
>
> And Thingol, in contempt and cunning, set the price: *"Bring me a Silmaril
> from Morgoth's crown. Then, and only then, may you call my daughter bride."*
>
> He meant it as a death sentence. A jest. A cruel impossibility to rid himself
> of a mortal nuisance. He did not know -- could not know -- that he was
> setting in motion the greatest quest of the Age, that the Silmaril he
> demanded in scorn would be won in love, and that the price of the winning
> would be his daughter's immortality.
>
> You sing of a father's pride and a daughter's defiance, and the Lament
> carries the weight of both.
>
> Mandos listens. Two lights burn in the grey halls.

---

#### Verse 3 -- The Faithful Who Followed (3 Silmarils)

> A third light. The Lament finds its grief.
>
> You sing of Finrod Felagund, noblest of the Noldor, who long ago swore an
> oath of friendship to Barahir -- Beren's father -- who saved his life in
> the Dagor Bragollach. When Beren came to Nargothrond bearing his father's
> ring, Finrod remembered. He rose from his throne. He laid down his crown.
> He called for companions.
>
> Ten answered. Edrahil was first.
>
> They went in disguise into the north, toward Sauron's isle, and were
> betrayed by sorcery. Sauron challenged Finrod to a duel of songs -- power
> against power, light against shadow. Finrod sang of Valinor, of the light
> of the Trees, of the love of Iluvatar. Sauron sang of treachery, of
> broken oaths, of the blood at Alqualonde.
>
> Finrod fell. The disguise was stripped. They were cast into darkness.
>
> One by one the wolves came. One by one the companions were taken. Edrahil
> was last. He did not cry out. Finrod heard the silence and knew.
>
> And when the wolf came for Beren, Finrod broke his chains with a strength
> born of love and oath, and fought the beast with bare hands, and killed it,
> and died. A king who died for a mortal man. A friend who kept his word.
>
> You sing of faithfulness, and the Halls of Mandos have never heard
> a truer note.
>
> Mandos listens. His silence changes.

---

#### Verse 4 -- The Tower Broken by Song (4 Silmarils)

> A fourth light. The Lament gathers strength.
>
> You sing of yourself -- not in pride but in truth.
>
> Of a maiden who would not wait. Who escaped her father's prison by weaving
> shadow from her own dark hair. Who walked into the wild with nothing but her
> voice and her courage and a hound that chose to follow.
>
> Of the isle of wolves, where Sauron sat in a tower of malice and the
> dungeons were full of the dead. Of Huan, the great hound, who seized the
> dark lord by the throat in wolf-form and held him until the tower shook.
> Of your own voice, rising above the battle, commanding:
>
> *"Yield, thou servant of Morgoth! Yield thy tower and thy power, or I
> shall strip thee bare and send thy quivering spirit back to Morgoth to
> face his scorn!"*
>
> And the Lord of Wolves yielded. And the tower fell. And the prisoners were
> freed -- those few who still drew breath.
>
> You sing of a power that is not force. Your mother Melian gave you the
> blood of the Ainur, and in that blood runs the music that shaped the world.
> When you sing, you do not compel. You *reveal*. You show things as they
> truly are, stripped of shadow and pretense, and in that truth even the
> mightiest darkness cannot stand.
>
> Mandos listens. He knows this power. It is akin to his own.

---

#### Verse 5 -- Before the Iron Crown (5 Silmarils)

> A fifth light. The Lament reaches the throne of darkness.
>
> You sing of Angband.
>
> Of the long road north, past the ruins of Sauron's tower, through the
> desert of Anfauglith where the bones of the Eldar lay like white stones
> beneath the ashen sky. Of the gates of Angband, where Carcharoth the
> great wolf crouched, fed on the malice of Morgoth himself, the mightiest
> beast that ever walked the world.
>
> Of the moment when you stood before the throne of Morgoth Bauglir, the
> Black Enemy, the fallen Vala, the being of greatest power in all of Arda,
> and you were afraid -- yes, even you, Tinuviel, daughter of a Maia -- but
> you sang anyway.
>
> You sang of beauty. Of the springs of Ivrin. Of the hemlock glade. Of
> starlight and laughter and the sound of Beren's voice saying *Tinuviel* for
> the first time. You sang of everything Morgoth sought to destroy, and the
> beauty of it was so great that the Lord of Darkness, who had marred the
> Music of the Ainur and stolen the light of the Trees and broken the peace
> of the world, could not withstand it. His eyes grew heavy. His iron crown
> tilted upon his vast brow. He slid from his throne.
>
> Morgoth slept. And Beren, with a knife of dwarven craft, cut a Silmaril
> from the Iron Crown while the Enemy dreamed.
>
> You sing of that moment -- the most audacious theft in the history of Arda
> -- and the Silmaril's light flares in the grey halls of Mandos, and for an
> instant the tapestries blaze with colour, and the Doomsman's eyes widen.

---

#### Verse 6 -- The Hand and the Wolf (6 Silmarils)

> A sixth light. The Lament reaches its sorrow.
>
> You sing of the flight from Angband. Of the gate where Carcharoth waited.
> Of Beren raising the Silmaril before the wolf's eyes, trusting in its holy
> light to ward against the beast.
>
> But the Silmaril's light only enraged the wolf. Carcharoth lunged.
> His jaws closed. Beren's hand -- the hand that held the jewel, the hand
> that had touched your face, the hand that reached for you in the hemlock
> glade -- was bitten clean away.
>
> The Silmaril burned within Carcharoth's belly and drove him mad, and he
> fled howling into the south, and the world trembled at his passage.
>
> And Beren fell.
>
> You carried him. Eagles came. Somehow -- through grace, through the last
> strength of the Ainur in your blood, through love that would not surrender
> -- you brought him back to Doriath. And there the Hunting of the Wolf was
> declared: Thingol and Beren and Huan and Beleg and Mablung, into the
> forest after the maddened beast.
>
> Carcharoth was slain. Huan killed him and was killed. The Silmaril was cut
> from the wolf's belly. Beren held it one last time -- in his remaining
> hand -- and placed it in your father's palm.
>
> *"Now is the quest achieved,"* he said. *"And my doom is full-wrought."*
>
> And he died. In your arms. Beneath the beeches of Doriath. And the
> nightingale sang no more.
>
> You sing of this, and the grief is so vast that the Halls of Mandos
> shudder, and the tapestries weep, and Mandos himself turns aside.

---

#### Verse 7 -- Love Stronger Than Death (7 Silmarils -- VICTORY)

> The seventh light blazes. The Lament is complete.
>
> You sing the final verse -- not of the past but of the *now*. Of this
> moment. Of a spirit standing in the grey halls, singing to a judge who has
> never been moved, asking for the one thing that has never been granted:
> a reversal of death itself.
>
> *I do not ask for what is easy,* you sing.
> *I do not ask for what is fair.*
> *I ask for what is true:*
> *that love does not end at the borders of the world.*
>
> *You have seen the proofs. Seven lights recovered from the deepest dark*
> *by the hands of those I loved -- not kings, not legends, but a hunter*
> *and a hound, a minstrel and a soldier, a shy elf-woman and a mortal*
> *outlaw. They carried my voice into the shadow, and the shadow yielded.*
>
> *If the least of heroes, armed with nothing but love, can bring light*
> *from the Enemy's crown --*
> *then surely love can bring one soul from death.*
>
> *This is my plea. This is my proof. This is my Lament.*
>
> *Hear me, Doomsman. And be moved.*
>
> Silence.
>
> The seven lights burn in the grey halls like seven stars. The tapestries
> of Vaire shimmer with colours they have never held: gold and green and the
> deep blue of twilight over Neldoreth.
>
> Mandos speaks. And his voice is changed.
>
> Not softer -- he is Namo, the Doomsman, and softness is not in his nature.
> But *deeper*. As though something beneath the stone has shifted.
> As though a foundation has been laid where none existed before.
>
> *"Tinuviel."*
>
> It is the first time he has spoken your name without formality.
>
> *"I have heard. And I am moved. And this has never happened before in all
> the ages of the world."*

### Defeat Path Chapters (Type 1: Hero Deaths)

These chapters trigger as the cumulative death count rises. They represent the
Lament faltering under the weight of loss -- the song wavering, the voice
breaking -- but never stopping. Luthien never stops. That is the point. That
is who she is.

Unlike the Glorfindel scenario, deaths here do not move toward a "game over."
They move toward *exhaustion* -- the increasing difficulty of singing through
grief, the voice growing hoarse, the melody fraying. But the voice does not
cease.

Mandos's responses grow colder and more formal with each death -- not from
cruelty but from the nature of his office. He is the keeper of the dead. Each
death added to his halls is another weight on the scales he must balance.

---

#### Death 1 -- The First Silence

> The song falters. A note goes wrong. A hero has fallen in the dark, and the
> verse that gave them shape unravels like thread pulled from a tapestry.
>
> Mandos speaks, and his voice is the voice of inevitability:
>
> *"One has fallen. The shadow is deep, Tinuviel, deeper than thy voice can
> reach. But thy song is not ended. Sing again."*
>
> You draw breath. You sing again.

---

#### Death 2 -- The Weight of Failure

> Another falls. The second verse of loss joins the first, a minor chord
> beneath the melody, a dissonance that threatens to overwhelm the song.
>
> Mandos does not speak. He watches. His eyes are grey as the halls he keeps,
> grey as the stone that does not weep.
>
> You sing. The song carries the weight. For now.

---

#### Death 3 -- The Fraying Thread

> A third death. The melody thins. You can feel the Lament losing its hold
> on the grey air, the verses dimming, the shapes you sang into being growing
> translucent and uncertain.
>
> Mandos speaks:
>
> *"Three have fallen. The Enemy's darkness swallows much, and even a voice
> such as thine cannot light every shadow. Consider, Tinuviel: is thy love
> worth such cost?"*
>
> You do not answer with words. You answer with the song. The song says:
> *Yes. Always yes.*

---

#### Death 4 -- Stone Does Not Weep

> The fourth death echoes through the Halls. The tapestries dim. The grey
> deepens toward darkness.
>
> You feel the curses settling on the song like frost on petals -- each
> death a weight that bows the melody closer to silence.
>
> Mandos watches. He does not weep. He has never wept.
>
> But his silence grows heavier.

---

#### Death 5 -- The Song of Wounded Things

> Five lost. The Lament is changing. It was a song of love; now it is a song
> of love *and* pain, and the two themes tangle like roots in dark earth,
> and the beauty of it is terrible, and the terror of it is beautiful.
>
> Mandos speaks:
>
> *"Thy song grows stronger in its sorrow, Tinuviel. That is the nature of
> the deepest music: grief refines what joy alone cannot temper. But strength
> born of sorrow has its limits. Even thine."*
>
> You sing. The limits are not yet reached.

---

#### Death 6 -- The Hound's Howl

> Six. The word is a wound. Six of the beloved have fallen in the dark, and
> their voices are added to the silence of the Halls, and you feel each one
> like a string cut from a harp.
>
> The song continues. It is thinner now. More fragile. But it continues.
>
> In the distance, you hear a sound that might be a howl -- Huan, the
> faithful hound, mourning across the divide between death and song. Or
> perhaps it is only the wind in the grey halls.
>
> There is no wind in the grey halls.

---

#### Death 7 -- The Doomsman's Doubt

> Seven lost. The number is significant. Seven lights you were asked to bring.
> Seven souls have been spent in the attempt.
>
> Mandos speaks, and for the first time there is something in his voice that
> is not judgment:
>
> *"Seven have fallen. The same number I set as thy proof. Consider the
> symmetry, Tinuviel: for every light recovered, a life is spent. The scales
> do not tip. They tremble. Is this love, or is it merely stubbornness?"*
>
> You answer him with the next verse of the Lament, and the verse says:
> *There is no difference.*

---

#### Death 8 -- The Thread Holds

> Eight. The Lament is wire-thin now, a single thread of melody stretched
> across the grey void. One more death and surely it will snap, surely the
> voice will fail, surely even Tinuviel's love has a breaking point.
>
> But it does not snap. The thread holds. The song continues.
>
> Mandos says nothing. He watches the thread, and his expression is
> unreadable.

---

#### Death 9 -- Where Do They Go?

> Nine. You feel the mortal deaths differently from the others. When an
> Elf falls, their spirit comes to these very halls -- you can sense them,
> faint presences in the grey, joining the silent company. But when a mortal
> falls...
>
> The mortal spirits pass *through*. Through the Halls, through the walls,
> through the very foundations of the world, into the fate of Men that even
> Mandos does not know.
>
> You sing after them. Your voice cannot follow.
>
> This is the wound at the center of the Lament: the unbridgeable distance
> between Elf and Man. This is why you are here. This is what you are trying
> to overcome.

---

#### Death 10 -- The Voice That Will Not Stop

> Ten. The song should have ended long ago. Any other singer would have
> faltered, would have wept, would have accepted the silence.
>
> But you are Luthien Tinuviel, daughter of Thingol and Melian, and the
> blood of the Ainur runs in your veins, and the Ainur made the world with
> music, and music does not stop until the singer chooses silence.
>
> You do not choose silence. You choose the next note. And the next. And
> the next.
>
> Mandos watches. Something in his grey eyes has changed.

---

#### Death 11 -- The Cost

> Eleven. The curses weigh like chains. Each hero who enters the dark carries
> the weight of every death before them, and the weight grows, and the songs
> grow harder, and the darkness grows darker.
>
> Mandos speaks:
>
> *"The cost is high, Tinuviel. Higher than any love should bear. And yet
> thou singest still. I begin to wonder if thou art mad, or if madness and
> love are the same thing, seen from different sides."*
>
> The song answers: *Both. Neither. It does not matter. I will not stop.*

---

#### Death 12 -- The Almost-Silence

> Twelve. The song is almost inaudible now -- a whisper, a breath, the ghost
> of a melody that once shook the Halls of Mandos. The tapestries have gone
> nearly dark. The lights already gathered dim and flicker.
>
> But "almost" is not "entirely." And a whisper can carry as far as a shout,
> if the listener leans close enough.
>
> Mandos leans.

---

#### Death 13 -- Beyond Reason

> Thirteen deaths, and the song continues. It is beyond reason now. Beyond
> sense. Beyond anything the Halls have ever witnessed. A voice that refuses
> to stop singing in a place built for silence.
>
> Mandos speaks:
>
> *"I have watched the fates of all who ever lived and died. I have heard
> the pleas of kings and the weeping of queens and the screaming of the
> damned. None of them sang for this long. None of them refused to stop."*
>
> A pause.
>
> *"Continue, Tinuviel. I am... listening."*

---

#### Death 14 -- The Stubborn Note

> Fourteen. You cling to a single note -- one note, held against the dark,
> against the weight, against the silence that presses in from every side.
> One note. One voice. One love.
>
> It is enough. It has always been enough.

---

#### Death 15+ -- The Song Endures

> The deaths mount. The curses deepen. The darkness presses. But the song
> endures, because the singer is Luthien, and Luthien does not yield, not to
> Sauron, not to Morgoth, not to her father's wrath, not to death itself.
>
> The Halls of Mandos have stood since the shaping of the world. They have
> never heard a song this long. They have never heard a love this stubborn.
>
> Mandos waits. The song continues.
>
> The song always continues.

---

### Special Chapter: Gift of Iluvatar (Mortal Hero Death)

> A mortal spirit passes through these halls and beyond -- beyond the stone,
> beyond the walls of the world, beyond Mandos's keeping, into the fate of
> Men that even the Valar do not know.
>
> Mandos speaks:
>
> *"This one I cannot hold. The Gift of Iluvatar takes the Secondborn beyond
> my reach. Thy voice, Tinuviel, cannot follow where this soul goes."*
>
> And this -- *this* -- is the wound the Lament was born to sing. This
> unbridgeable distance. This gulf between Elf and Man that no power in Arda
> can cross.
>
> Except, perhaps, love.
>
> The song does not follow the mortal spirit. But it reaches after it, and
> the reaching is the most beautiful part of the Lament, and Mandos, who
> knows all fates, does not know where the reaching ends.

## VI. Hero Victory and Defeat Messages

Each message is written from Luthien's perspective as she sings. Victory
messages (with Silmaril) represent a verse of the Lament sung in full -- the
hero's face illuminated by the recovered light, the proof laid before Mandos.
Defeat messages (without Silmaril) represent a verse that falters -- the
memory dimming, the melody breaking, but never ceasing.

---

### 1. Beren

**Victory:**

> *My love.* The Silmaril's light falls upon your face, and I see you as you
> were in the hemlock glade: ragged, road-worn, with leaves in your dark hair
> and wonder in your grey eyes. You called me Tinuviel and did not know what
> you had done. You changed the world with a name. You reached into Morgoth's
> crown and took back the light. You lost your hand and did not flinch. You
> held the Silmaril and said *"Now is the quest achieved"* and meant it, and
> died, and left me here in the grey halls singing for your return. I sing
> of you, Beren. I will always sing of you. The light proves what I have
> always known: that your love was real, and mine was real, and no darkness
> in all of Arda can make that untrue.

**Defeat:**

> *My love.* I reach for your face in the song, but the darkness is too
> deep, and the verse unravels, and you slip away as you slipped away on the
> grass of Doriath with the venom of the wolf in your veins. I will find you.
> I will sing you back into the light. Not this verse -- but the next. Or the
> next. Or the next after that. I will not stop, Beren. I did not stop for
> Sauron. I did not stop for Morgoth. I will not stop for death.

---

### 2. Finrod Felagund

**Victory:**

> *Noble friend.* You who kept your oath when all others wavered. You who laid
> down your crown for a mortal man's quest because his father once saved your
> life. In the light of this Silmaril I see you clearly: golden-haired, gentle,
> the noblest of the Noldor, who asked nothing of the world but the chance to
> keep his word. You dueled Sauron in songs of power and fell. You wrestled a
> werewolf in chains and won, and the winning killed you. They will remember
> you as Finrod Felagund, Friend of Men. I remember you as Finrod who followed
> love into the dark and did not come back. This light is partly yours. This
> verse of the Lament is your monument.

**Defeat:**

> *Noble friend.* The dungeon claims you again. The wolves come in the dark,
> and your chains are heavy, and your song is not enough -- it was not enough
> against Sauron, and it is not enough now. But Finrod -- you taught me
> something: that keeping an oath can be more powerful than winning a battle.
> You lost and still you conquered. The next verse will carry your light
> further. I promise.

---

### 3. Daeron

**Victory:**

> *Singer.* My minstrel. My betrayer. My mirror. You loved me, Daeron, and
> I know the weight of that, because I know what it is to love and be unable
> to reach the beloved. You told my father when Beren came. You told him again
> when I sought to follow. Twice you betrayed me, and both times out of love,
> and that is the cruelest kind of betrayal -- the kind that cannot be hated.
> The Silmaril's light falls on your face and I see the tears you shed when
> I left Doriath, and I hear the songs you sang alone in the eastern
> wilderness after I was gone. We are alike, you and I: singers whose songs
> are grief. But my grief has a purpose. I am singing Beren back from death.
> What are you singing for, Daeron? In the light, I hope you find your answer.

**Defeat:**

> *Singer.* Your music fades in the darkness, and I remember the halls of
> Menegroth where your voice and mine wove together, two themes, two griefs,
> two loves that could never meet. You wander somewhere in the east, beyond
> all finding, singing songs no one hears. We are both lost. But I will
> find my way back. I wish I could say the same for you.

---

### 4. Beleg

**Victory:**

> *Hunter.* Strongbow. Guardian of the Girdle, tireless warden of Doriath's
> borders, who walked the paths of my father's realm in silence and moonlight,
> keeping watch so that those within could sleep in peace. I knew you as a
> shadow between the trees -- always present, never intrusive, the guardian
> who asked for no thanks. The Silmaril's light shows me your bow drawn and
> your eyes steady, and I think: this is what faithfulness looks like. Not
> oaths or crowns or grand gestures. Just a hunter in the forest, doing what
> he was born to do, for those he was born to protect. This light is proof
> that the faithful matter. You matter, Beleg.

**Defeat:**

> *Hunter.* The darkness takes you, and the forest loses its warden, and the
> paths of Doriath grow cold. You will fall again -- not here, but in the
> tale that has not yet been told, where you will follow Turin into shadows
> even darker than these, and your faithfulness will be repaid with a sword
> in the dark. I sing of you, Beleg. I sing of the faithful who are never
> thanked enough.

---

### 5. Mablung

**Victory:**

> *Captain.* Of the Heavy Hand. You were there at the Hunting of the Wolf,
> beside Beren and my father and Huan. You were the steady one -- the soldier
> who does not seek glory, who holds the line because someone must. When
> Carcharoth raged and the world shook, you did not flinch. You served. The
> Silmaril's light falls on your scarred hands, and I sing of duty -- the
> quiet kind, the unglamorous kind, the kind that keeps kingdoms standing.
> You are not mentioned in the great songs. But you are in mine.

**Defeat:**

> *Captain.* You fall, and the line breaks, and the duty is left unfulfilled.
> But duty does not die with the soldier. It passes to the next. And the
> next. And the next. That is what duty means: it is larger than any one
> hand, even a heavy one. I will sing you into the dark again, Mablung.
> The duty endures.

---

### 6. Celeborn

**Victory:**

> *Kinsman.* Prince of Doriath, tall and silver, who will outlive my father's
> kingdom and my mother's enchantment and the very Age that bore us. In the
> Silmaril's light I see you among the beeches of our home -- the white trees
> of Menegroth, the silver river, the halls carved from living stone. I sing
> of Doriath. I sing of the realm I left behind when I chose to follow Beren
> into the wild. The kingdom endures, for now. You endure. And in my song,
> the Doriath I loved endures forever -- unbroken, unsacked, the Girdle
> still strong, the nightingale still singing in the hemlock glade.

**Defeat:**

> *Kinsman.* You fall, and Doriath trembles. The silver trees bend. The halls
> go dark. But you will rise again -- you always do, Celeborn. You are the
> one who endures, through sorrow and ruin and the long ages of the world.
> If my song fails, you will still stand. That knowledge is a comfort even
> the Lament cannot equal.

---

### 7. Barahir

**Victory:**

> *Father of my beloved.* I never knew you. You died before Beren wandered
> into Doriath, slain in the wilderness of Dorthonion, betrayed by one of
> your own men. But I know you through your son -- through the set of his
> jaw, the stubbornness of his love, the ring he wore upon his hand. Finrod's
> ring. The ring you won when you saved the Elven-king's life in the Dagor
> Bragollach. That ring is the thread that binds your fate to mine: your
> valor saved Finrod, Finrod's oath saved Beren, and Beren's love saved me
> from a life without meaning. In the Silmaril's light, I see the chain of
> loyalty that led from your hand to mine, and I sing of fathers whose
> children complete the work they began.

**Defeat:**

> *Father of my beloved.* You fall in the dark as you fell in Dorthonion,
> and the ring passes to no one, and the chain of loyalty snaps. But it will
> be forged again. I am the daughter-in-law you never met, and I am singing
> your name before the Doomsman of the Valar, and in my song your hand still
> holds the ring, and the ring still shines.

---

### 8. Huan

**Victory:**

> *Faithful one.* My dear, dear hound. In the Silmaril's light I see you as
> you were when you first came to me -- great as a horse, grey as mist,
> with eyes wiser than any beast's eyes should be. You chose me. Not from
> command -- Celegorm was your master, and he was cruel, and you knew it, and
> you chose *me*. You held Sauron by the throat until the tower fell. You
> walked with me to the gates of Angband. You fought Carcharoth, the
> mightiest wolf that ever lived, and killed him, and were killed, and with
> your last breath spoke your last of three allotted words: *"Farewell,
> Tinuviel."*
>
> I cannot say farewell. Not yet. Not while the song continues. This light
> is yours, Huan. Faithful one. Best of all companions. I will never forget
> the sound of your voice.

**Defeat:**

> *Faithful one.* You fall, and the howl that echoes through the halls is
> grief made sound. Three times in your life you were permitted to speak,
> and all three times you spoke of love or duty or farewell. In the dark
> you have no voice. But in my song, you speak forever. Rest, Huan. I will
> call you again.

---

### 9. Edrahil

**Victory:**

> *Chief of the faithful.* You cast down your ring before Finrod's throne
> and said you would follow, though every other heart in Nargothrond had
> turned cold. Nine followed your example. Ten companions into the dark.
> You did not come back. In the Silmaril's light I see your face -- not a
> king's face, not a hero's face, just a soldier's face, loyal and
> unremarkable and utterly brave. They will not name you in the great songs.
> They will speak of Finrod and Beren and perhaps of the ten who followed.
> But I name you, Edrahil. In my Lament, before the Doomsman of the Valar,
> your name is spoken, and it rings as true as any king's.

**Defeat:**

> *Chief of the faithful.* The wolves take you again, as they took you in
> Sauron's dungeon, and the silence after your death is the same silence
> Finrod heard and wept for. You are one of the unnamed. The forgotten. The
> faithful who follow and are not remembered. But I remember. My song
> remembers. The next verse will carry your courage further.

---

### 10. Nellas

**Victory:**

> *Watcher.* Little one. Shy one. Who tended the forest's edge and watched
> over a child from the branches without him ever knowing. You trembled
> before my father when they asked you to speak, and you spoke anyway,
> because the truth was more important than your fear. In the Silmaril's
> light I see you clearly: small, wide-eyed, leaf-crowned, the least of
> heroes, the kindest of souls. And I think -- if your small hands can carry
> this light from the deepest dark, then my argument before Mandos is proved
> beyond all doubt. Not by kings. Not by legends. By you, Nellas. By the
> gentlest and most ordinary heart in all of Doriath.
>
> Love does not need to be mighty. It only needs to be real.

**Defeat:**

> *Watcher.* You fall in the dark, and the trees of Doriath lose one of
> their small guardians, and the children of the realm sleep less safely
> tonight. You are the least of my heroes, Nellas. And the most important.
> Because if even you can be brave, then bravery is not a gift of the great
> but a choice of the small. I will sing you back. I will sing you braver
> than you believe yourself to be. And you will go into the dark again, and
> the dark will not know what to do with such quiet courage.

## VII. Final Scene -- The Choice

**This scene plays when 7 Silmarils have been recovered.**

It consists of three parts: The Judgment, The Choice, and The Return.

The total score across all runs determines the quality of the ending -- not
whether Beren is returned (he always is; this is Luthien's story and she
always succeeds) but the *manner* of the mercy and the depth of Mandos's
response.

---

### Part 1: The Judgment (Always Plays)

> Seven lights burn in the Halls of Mandos. Seven proofs of love recovered
> from the deepest dark by the hands of those you loved. The Lament is
> complete.
>
> The tapestries of Vaire blaze with color -- gold and green and silver and
> the deep twilight blue of Neldoreth, and the crimson of Beren's blood, and
> the white of Huan's fur, and the grey of your own eyes when you danced
> beneath the hemlocks and the world was young.
>
> Mandos stands before you. He is taller than the mountains. His eyes hold
> every death that ever was or will be. His voice shaped the Doom of the
> Noldor. His judgment has never been questioned.
>
> He speaks.
>
> *"Tinuviel."*
>
> The word falls like a stone into still water.
>
> *"I set a challenge. Seven lights from the deepest dark, to prove that
> love endures where all else fails. Thou hast brought them. Through the
> hands of a mortal man and an Elven prince and a hound of Valinor and a
> shy wood-elf and a soldier and a singer and a hunter and a captain and
> the father of thy beloved and the chief of the faithful ten."*
>
> *"Not kings. Not legends. Ordinary souls, armed with nothing but thy
> voice and their own small courage."*
>
> *"I have watched the fates of all who lived. I have heard the pleas of
> Feanor, the proudest. I have recorded the doom of Morgoth, the mightiest.
> I have kept the count of every death since the first Elf opened his eyes
> beneath the stars."*
>
> *"And never -- in all the ages of the world -- has anyone sung as thou
> hast sung. And never has anyone proved what thou hast proved."*

---

### Part 2: The Choice

*The following text adjusts based on the campaign's total score.*

---

#### If total score is HIGH (few deaths, strong performance):

> Mandos is silent for a time that might be moments or ages.
>
> Then he does something that has never happened before in the history of
> Arda, and will never happen again:
>
> He turns from his seat of judgment and goes to Manwe.
>
> What passes between them is not told. What word comes from Iluvatar himself
> is hidden beyond the knowledge of the wise. But when Mandos returns, his
> face is... not changed. The Doomsman does not change. But there is something
> in his eyes that was not there before. Something that might, in a lesser
> being, be called wonder.
>
> *"Tinuviel. A doom such as has never been spoken is set before thee. Two
> roads."*
>
> *"The first: go to Valimar, and dwell among the Valar in the Blessed Realm,
> and all thy grief shall be healed, and thy years shall be without number,
> and thou shalt forget the mortal man and the pain and the song and the
> shadow, and rest in light forever."*
>
> *"The second: return to Middle-earth, and Beren with thee. But both shall
> be mortal. The life of the Eldar shall leave thee. Thou shalt know age and
> weariness and death, and pass beyond the circles of the world into the
> fate of Men, and even I shall not know where thou goest."*
>
> He pauses.
>
> *"This doom is without precedent. No Elf has ever been offered mortality.
> No mortal has ever been recalled from death. What thou hast done with thy
> song has bent the very fabric of Doom, and I, who am the keeper of Doom,
> do not fully understand how."*
>
> *"Choose, Tinuviel."*
>
> You do not hesitate.
>
> You have never hesitated. Not when your father locked you in Hirilorn.
> Not when Sauron's wolves came. Not when Morgoth sat enthroned above you.
> Not when Carcharoth bit Beren's hand from his wrist.
>
> You choose Beren. You choose mortality. You choose love.
>
> *"I choose him. I choose the brief flame over the eternal starlight. I
> choose the road that leads beyond the world, because he walks it, and I
> will not let him walk it alone."*
>
> Mandos nods. And in that nod -- the smallest gesture the Doomsman of the
> Valar has ever made -- there is something that has no name in any language
> of Elves or Men. Something that the Ainulindale did not contain. Something
> new.
>
> Perhaps it is respect.
>
> Perhaps it is awe.
>
> Perhaps -- and this is the thought that will be whispered in Valinor for
> ages to come, though none can confirm it -- perhaps it is the faintest,
> most unprecedented ghost of a tear in the eye of the being who has never
> wept.

---

#### If total score is MEDIUM (moderate deaths):

> Mandos is silent.
>
> Then he goes to Manwe, and what passes between them is not told.
>
> When he returns, his voice is grave -- graver than before, which should
> not be possible, for he is the gravest being in Arda.
>
> *"Tinuviel. Thy song has moved me, and that has never happened. But the
> cost was high. Many fell in the proving. The shadow took much before the
> light was won."*
>
> *"Two roads are set before thee."*
>
> *"The first: Valimar, and healing, and forgetfulness of all grief."*
>
> *"The second: Middle-earth, and Beren, and mortality. Both shall be mortal.
> Both shall die. And the years given you shall be fewer than you hope."*
>
> *"The proving was... sufficient. But the wounds upon the song are deep, and
> the mercy is tempered by the weight of what was spent to earn it."*
>
> *"Choose."*
>
> You choose as you always choose. As you always will choose.
>
> *"I choose him."*
>
> Mandos nods. There is no tear. There is no wonder. But there is
> acknowledgment -- the Doomsman recognizing that a thing has been done that
> he did not believe possible, and that the doing of it, however imperfect,
> has earned its reward.
>
> *"Go, then, Tinuviel. Go and be mortal. Go and be brief. Go and be loved."*

---

#### If total score is LOW (many deaths, long struggle):

> Mandos is silent for a very long time.
>
> When he speaks, his voice is cold. Not cruel -- Mandos is never cruel. But
> cold, as the stone of his halls is cold, as the truth is cold when it
> carries no comfort.
>
> *"Tinuviel. Thou hast brought the seven lights. The challenge is met. But
> the cost..."*
>
> He does not finish the sentence. He does not need to. The deaths weigh in
> the air like a winter that will not end.
>
> He goes to Manwe. He returns.
>
> *"Two roads. Valimar and peace. Or Middle-earth and Beren and death."*
>
> *"I will not embellish the choice. The mercy is granted, for thou hast
> earned it, though the earning nearly broke thee. But know this: the years
> given will be brief. The mortality will be swift. The price of what was
> spent to prove thy love will be measured in the shortness of the time
> remaining."*
>
> *"Choose."*
>
> You choose. Of course you choose. You have always chosen. You will always
> choose. If the years are brief, they will be together. If the road is
> short, you will walk it hand in hand. If death comes swiftly, you will
> meet it singing.
>
> *"I choose him. I will always choose him."*
>
> Mandos nods. Just once.
>
> *"Go."*

### Part 3: The Return (Always Plays)

> You walk out of the grey halls.
>
> The road is long. The road between death and life is the longest road in
> Arda, longer than the Helcaraxe, longer than the road from Valinor to
> Angband, longer than the road Beren walked through Nan Dungortheb where
> the spiders dwell. But you walk it, because Beren is at the end of it,
> and that is enough.
>
> You walk, and as you walk, your spirit finds a body again -- not the
> body you left in Doriath, radiant and ageless, the fairest thing beneath
> the sky. A mortal body. One that will age and weaken and finally fail.
> One that carries within it the seed of its own ending.
>
> You do not mourn the immortality. You chose this. You would choose it
> again. A thousand years of starlit peace in Valimar is nothing beside
> one mortal lifetime with Beren's hand in yours.
>
> And there he is.
>
> Standing on the green hill of Tol Galen, in the land of Ossiriand where
> the rivers run clear and the trees are ancient and the world has not yet
> learned the full measure of its grief. Standing in the sunlight with both
> hands -- *both* hands -- restored, whole, alive.
>
> He sees you. He says the word he said in the hemlock glade, the word that
> started everything:
>
> *"Tinuviel."*
>
> You run to him. The grass is warm beneath your feet. The sun is on your
> face. Somewhere in the trees a nightingale begins to sing, and the song
> is not a lament.
>
> For now, in this moment, on this green hill, in this brief mortal life
> you have chosen, the nightingale sings of joy.
>
> *Among the tales of sorrow and of ruin that come down to us from the*
> *darkness of those days there are yet some in which amid weeping there*
> *is joy and under the shadow of death light that endures. And of these*
> *histories most fair still in the ears of the Elves is the tale of*
> *Beren and Luthien.*
>
> The song of Luthien before Mandos was the song most fair that ever in
> words was woven, and the song most sorrowful that ever the world shall
> hear. Unchanged, imperishable, it is sung still in Valinor beyond the
> hearing of the world.
>
> And listening, the Valar grieve.

## VIII. Implementation Notes (For Future Coding)

### Campaign Structure
- **Run count:** Unlimited (Silmaril-gated, not run-count-based)
- **Victory trigger:** 7 Silmarils recovered across all runs
- **Defeat trigger:** None. The campaign continues until victory.
- **Score tracking:** Sum of all individual run scores
- **Hero pool:** Restricted to 10 specific characters (3 new + 7 existing)
- **Hero reuse:** Allow same hero in multiple runs
- **No quest system:** Unlike Glorfindel, this scenario has no Valar quests
  or bindable oaths. The simplicity is intentional: no external aid, no divine
  bonus, only the song and the heroes it shapes.

### Narrative Progression
- Opening Scene: Fires once at campaign creation
- Victory Chapters (Type 0): Trigger by cumulative Silmaril count (1-7)
- Defeat Chapters (Type 1): Trigger by cumulative death count (1-15+)
- Special Mortality Chapter: Triggers when a mortal hero (Beren, Barahir) dies,
  alongside or replacing the standard defeat chapter
- Hero Victory/Defeat Messages: Trigger on run completion, indexed by hero ID
- Final Scene: Triggers when 7th Silmaril is recovered

### Curse Economy
- **Base curse:** Each death adds stat penalties more severe than Glorfindel
  scenario
- **No blessings:** Unlike Glorfindel, there is no blessing system to offset
  curses
- **No oaths:** No quest system means no oath bonuses
- **Practical difficulty ceiling:** After approximately 15-20 deaths, curses
  make Silmaril recovery extremely difficult for Power 0-1 heroes. This is the
  "soft defeat" -- not a game-over, but practical impossibility
- **Design intent:** The absence of blessings and oaths mirrors the scenario's
  theme. Luthien has no external aid. No Vala blesses her quest. Mandos is the
  judge, not the helper. The only power is the song itself.

### Creative Liberties (Tolkien-Compatible)
- Mandos setting a specific challenge (seven Silmarils) before hearing
  Luthien's full plea is original. In the Silmarillion, Mandos is moved by
  the song alone. The "challenge" is a gameplay conceit, but it is compatible
  with Mandos's character as the keeper of Doom who requires proof, not mere
  words. It also reflects the broader Tolkienian theme of divine testing
  (Aule with the Dwarves, Numenor with the Ban).

- The "heroes as verses of the Lament made flesh" conceit -- Luthien singing
  the memories of her loved ones into Angband -- is original but grounded in
  Tolkien's metaphysics. In the Ainulindale, reality IS music. The Ainur sang
  the world into being. Luthien, as half-Maia, has a connection to this
  primal creative force. Her song before Mandos is described as having power
  beyond any mortal art. The idea that her voice could give shape to memory
  is a natural extension of this power.

- The specific emotional texture of Mandos's responses (growing from cold
  formality to something approaching wonder) is invented, but consistent with
  the Silmarillion's statement that he was "moved to pity" -- implying a
  process of being moved, not an instantaneous capitulation.

- The "no defeat condition" is original and mechanically unprecedented, but it
  perfectly captures Luthien's character. In the Silmarillion, she never gives
  up. She escapes imprisonment. She walks into enemy strongholds. She sings
  before the greatest evil in the world. The idea that her song literally
  cannot be stopped is the purest expression of this nature.

- The score-based variation in Mandos's response (high/medium/low) is original.
  In the Silmarillion, the mercy is simply granted after the song. The idea
  that the "quality" of the mercy varies based on the cost of the proving is
  a gameplay extension that adds narrative weight to player performance without
  contradicting the canonical outcome (Luthien always chooses Beren; Beren
  always returns).

- Huan as a playable hero is mechanically unusual but narratively essential.
  Tolkien describes him as having intelligence, speech (three times), and moral
  agency (choosing Luthien over Celegorm). He is not a mere animal but a being
  of Valinor with his own fate and his own choices. His inclusion as a "hero"
  is a creative liberty, but one that honors Tolkien's characterization.

- Nellas's participation in Angband runs is ahistorical (she is a minor figure
  who never left Doriath's borders). However, in the scenario's conceit, the
  heroes are not literally entering Angband but are manifested by Luthien's
  song. Nellas's "entry" into Angband represents Luthien singing of the
  smallest and gentlest of her people -- the verse that proves love needs no
  greatness to endure.

- The emphasis on "ordinary heroes" as the core theme is original analysis,
  but it reflects Tolkien's own deepest conviction. In his letters, he wrote:
  "I think the simple 'rustic' love of Sam and his Rosie (nowhere elaborated)
  is absolutely essential to the study of his character, and to the theme of
  the relation of ordinary life... to quests, sacrifice, causes, and the
  'long defeat'." The Luthien scenario applies this same principle to the
  First Age: the ordinary people matter as much as the legends.

### Tolkien's Key Insight

The tale of Beren and Luthien was Tolkien's most personal story. He and his
wife Edith are buried beneath headstones reading "Beren" and "Luthien." He
wrote that the tale was inspired by Edith dancing for him in a woodland glade
during the First World War.

The scenario rests on Tolkien's most fundamental belief: that love transcends
death. Not metaphorically. Not symbolically. *Actually*. In the mythology he
created, one person's love was so powerful that it moved the immovable, bent
the unbendable, and reversed the irreversible. Luthien's song did not merely
express grief. It *changed reality*.

And the instrument of that change was not power or craft or force. It was
truth. Luthien sang what was true, and the truth was: *I love him, and I will
not stop, and no force in Arda can make me stop.*

That is the Lament. That is the scenario. That is the song that never ends.
