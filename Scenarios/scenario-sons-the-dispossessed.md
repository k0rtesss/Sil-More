# The Dispossessed: Seven Fires

## Scenario Design Document

---

## I. Lore Foundation

### The Death of Feanor — What He Saw Last

Feanor lies upon the ash-field within sight of Thangorodrim, dying of
Balrog-wounds. His seven sons have borne him back from the fight. They kneel
around him in a half-circle — Maedhros the tall, Maglor the singer, Celegorm
the fair, Caranthir the dark, Curufin who wears his father's face, and the
twins Amrod and Amras, still young, still bright.

Seven faces. The last things Feanor will ever see.

And in the clarity of death — that terrible gift granted to the greatest of
the Eldar — he sees not only their faces *now*, but their faces *then* and
*hence*. He sees what they were in Valinor, when they were children and the
Trees still shone. And he sees what they will become: kin-slayers, oath-
breakers, the Dispossessed, driven by his words through ruin after ruin until
every one of them is dead or worse than dead.

He sees all of it. Every kinslaying, every betrayal, every lonely end.

And he commands them to hold the Oath.

This is the deepest horror of the Feanorian tragedy: not that the father
was ignorant of what the Oath would cost, but that he *knew* — and chose it
anyway. Not out of madness. Not out of cruelty. But out of the same pride
that made the Silmarils: the absolute refusal to accept that anything he
made — jewels, sons, oaths — could be less than eternal.

### Relationship to Spirit of Fire

The first scenario (*Spirit of Fire*) is Feanor remembering the people who
**shaped** him — parents, wife, teacher, brothers, kin. He looks backward
through his life.

This scenario is Feanor looking at the people he **shaped** — his seven sons.
He looks forward through their lives. The dying clarity shows him everything:
the childhood he gave them, the gifts he kindled in them, the Oath he bound
them with, and the doom that Oath will bring.

Same ash-field. Same dying flame. But where Spirit of Fire asks "what made
Feanor?", this scenario asks "what did Feanor make?"

The answer is seven fires. And every one of them will go out.

### Key Lore Touchstones Referenced

- **The seven sons in Valinor** — Each was born in the Blessed Realm under the
  light of the Two Trees. Maedhros was named Maitimo ("well-shaped") and
  Nelyafinwe ("Finwe the third"). Maglor was Kanafinwe ("strong-voiced Finwe").
  Celegorm was Tyelkormo ("hasty-riser"), a companion of Orome. Caranthir was
  Morifinwe ("dark Finwe"). Curufin was Curufinwe ("skillful Finwe") — the only
  son to bear Feanor's own father-name. The twins were Pityafinwe and Telufinwe
  ("little Finwe" and "last Finwe"). (HoME XII)
- **Nerdanel's influence** — Maedhros and Maglor took more after their mother
  than their father, being gentler and more temperate. The younger sons grew
  steadily more like Feanor. (HoME XII)
- **The Oath sworn by all seven** — In Tirion, beneath the dying light of the
  Trees, all seven sons swore alongside their father. (Silmarillion Ch. 9)
- **Maedhros on Thangorodrim** — Captured, hung by his wrist, rescued by Fingon
  at the cost of his hand. Yielded the crown to Fingolfin. (Silmarillion Ch. 13)
- **Celegorm and Huan** — The wolfhound of Valinor, given by Orome, who chose
  Luthien over his master. (Silmarillion Ch. 19)
- **Curufin and Celebrimbor** — Curufin's own son renounced him and stayed in
  Nargothrond when his father was driven out. (Silmarillion Ch. 19)
- **The Second Kinslaying (Doriath)** — Celegorm, Caranthir, and Curufin fell.
  (Silmarillion Ch. 22)
- **The Third Kinslaying (Havens of Sirion)** — Amrod and Amras fell.
  (Silmarillion Ch. 24)
- **Maglor and the foster-sons** — After the Havens, Maglor took in Elrond and
  Elros, and "love grew between them, as little might be thought."
  (Silmarillion Ch. 24)
- **The stolen Silmarils** — Maedhros cast himself into a chasm of fire. Maglor
  cast his jewel into the Sea and wandered the shores forever.
  (Silmarillion Ch. 24)
- **The burning at Losgar** — In HoME XII, a late text suggests Amrod was
  sleeping on the ships when Feanor burned them.

## II. Scenario Overview

**Name:** The Dispossessed
**Subtitle:** Seven Fires
**Type:** Fast Campaign (run-count-based, not silmaril-gated)
**Runs:** 7 (for the seven sons of Feanor)
**End Condition:** After 7 runs, the campaign concludes regardless of outcome
**Scoring:** Total score across all 7 runs is compared
**Hero Pool:** 7 heroes — the seven sons of Feanor (all existing in character.txt)
**Difficulty:** Hard — all heroes carry KINSLAYER, most carry CURSE; thematically
the Dispossessed are doomed, and the gameplay reflects this. Scores should be
compared only against other Dispossessed campaigns, not against Spirit of Fire
or The Last Duel.

### Narrative Frame

The same ash-field as *Spirit of Fire*. The same dying Feanor. But where that
scenario looked backward through his life, this one looks *forward* through
his sons' lives.

Feanor lies broken, his body already beginning to burn from within, and his
dying eyes move from face to face. Seven sons. Seven flames he kindled in the
Blessed Realm. Death grants him sight beyond sight — the clarity that comes
only to the greatest of the Eldar in their final hour — and he sees not just
the sons who kneel before him but the men they will become, the deeds they
will commit, and the prices they will pay.

Each run is a son's life, unfolding in Feanor's vision. The player chooses a
son and descends into Angband — but the narrative frame is Feanor watching,
judging, grieving, and refusing to relent.

After seven runs — seven visions — the final scene fires: Feanor, having seen
everything, speaks the command.

### Progression Structure

| Run | Vision Theme | Narrative Focus |
|-----|-------------|-----------------|
| 1 | The Kindling | Remembering their births, childhood in Valinor's light |
| 2 | The Gifts | Each son's nature — craft, song, wrath, beauty, loyalty |
| 3 | The Swearing | The Oath spoken, seven voices answering his |
| 4 | The Crossing | What they did at Alqualonde and Losgar |
| 5 | The Sight | Death-vision: their future wars and alliances |
| 6 | The Unraveling | Death-vision: the Kinslayings, brother turning on kin |
| 7 | The Reckoning | Death-vision: each son's end — fire, sea, sword |

## IV. Narrative Script

### Opening Scene (Plays Once, at Campaign Start)

> **Title: Seven Faces in the Ash**
>
> The Balrog-fire is in you, eating inward, and you know with the
> certainty of the dying that this wound will not heal. Not because of
> Gothmog's flame — flame has never harmed the Spirit of Fire — but because
> your own spirit has burned too long and too fierce, and the body that
> housed it is spent.
>
> Your sons carry you. You feel their hands — how many hands? Fourteen,
> each one familiar, each one shaped by your blood and Nerdanel's patience.
> They lower you onto the grey earth within sight of Thangorodrim, and
> their faces swim above you, and you see them.
>
> Maedhros. Tallest, copper-haired, your father's brow above your mother's
> jaw. He holds your head, and his grip is steady, and you remember: he was
> your firstborn, and Nerdanel wept with joy, and the Trees were golden
> above the house in Tirion.
>
> Maglor. His lips are moving — singing, always singing, even now — and the
> melody is one you taught him when he was barely old enough to hold a note.
>
> Celegorm. Fair-faced, wild-eyed, smelling of hound and forest even here
> on the ash-field. Huan whimpers at his feet.
>
> Caranthir. Dark-browed, jaw clenched, refusing to weep. Your most
> stubborn son. Your most lonely.
>
> Curufin. *Your face.* He wears your face. Every time you look at him it
> is like looking into still water, and what you see frightens you, because
> you know what that face is capable of.
>
> And the twins. Amrod and Amras. Red-haired, wide-eyed, so young. They
> should not be here. They should be in Valinor, hunting in forests that
> never darken, growing old in peace. They should never have sworn the
> Oath. You should never have let them.
>
> But you did. You lit these seven fires, and now, dying, you see where
> each one will burn and how each one will go out.

### Vision Chapters (Run Progression — Type 0: Light)

These play at the START of each run, setting the vision's theme.

---

#### Run 1 — The Kindling

> **Title: Small Flames**
>
> The dying sight turns backward first, to Valinor, to the golden years
> when your sons were small and the world was whole.
>
> You remember Maedhros at three, climbing the wall of the forge and
> falling, and how you caught him with hands still black with soot, and he
> laughed instead of crying. You remember Maglor's first note — not his
> first word but his first *note*, a sound so pure that Nerdanel dropped
> her chisel and wept. You remember Celegorm returning from the woods
> with a fox-kit in his arms and a hound-pup at his heels, already
> Orome's creature.
>
> You remember Caranthir's first rage — a toy broken, a brother blamed —
> and how his small face darkened exactly as yours does, and Nerdanel
> looked at you with eyes that said: *you see what you are passing on?*
>
> Curufin at the forge. Five years old. Holding the hammer correctly
> without being taught.
>
> And the twins. Born together, sleeping together, finishing each other's
> sentences before they had sentences to finish. Two flames from one spark.
>
> Seven sons. Seven small flames in the light of the Trees. You made
> them. They are your finest work and your most terrible.

---

#### Run 2 — The Gifts

> **Title: Fire Takes Many Shapes**
>
> Each son carries a different facet of your flame, refracted through
> Nerdanel's tempering into forms you could not have predicted.
>
> Maedhros has your will but not your pride. He leads without commanding,
> and his brothers follow not from fear but from love. This frightens
> you, because love can be reasoned with, and the Oath cannot.
>
> Maglor has your craft but turns it to song instead of metal. His voice
> is the greatest instrument ever made by the Noldor, and he built it
> himself, note by note, in the quiet hours you filled with hammering.
>
> Celegorm has your fire but spills it into the wild — the hunt, the
> chase, the bright fierce joy of the body in motion. Of all your sons,
> he is the most alive. Of all your sons, he will fall the farthest.
>
> Caranthir has your wrath. Undiluted. Untempered by Nerdanel's patience.
> He will build kingdoms and destroy friendships with equal fervor.
>
> Curufin has your skill, your face, and your cunning. He is the mirror
> you cannot look away from. His own son will look at him one day and see
> what you see now, and walk away.
>
> The twins have your loyalty, distilled to its purest form: they follow
> because you lead. They will follow to the end.
>
> You made all of this. You are proud of all of it. And the Oath will
> ruin all of it.

---

#### Run 3 — The Swearing

> **Title: Seven Voices, One Doom**
>
> The Trees are dying. The darkness presses in. On the hill of Tuna you
> stand with a sword that reflects light that no longer exists, and the
> words come out of you like metal from a mold — inevitable, shaped by
> forces older than your grief, stronger than your will.
>
> *"Be he foe or friend — "*
>
> And you look at your sons' faces as they echo you. Maedhros, steady
> and grim. Maglor, his voice making even the Oath sound beautiful, and
> you hate him for it because beauty will make the words easier to
> remember. Celegorm, fierce and eager, not understanding what he is
> saying. Caranthir, speaking through clenched teeth, meaning every
> syllable. Curufin, watching you rather than the crowd, learning not
> the words but the *method*.
>
> And the twins. Their voices high and clear. Children's voices, swearing
> by the Everlasting Darkness.
>
> In this moment, dying, you understand what you did to them. You took
> seven fires that you kindled in love and bound them to a purpose they
> did not choose. A father's oath became a son's chain. And a chain
> forged by the greatest smith in Arda cannot be broken — not by love,
> not by wisdom, not by death itself.
>
> They swore because you asked. That is all. That is everything.

---

#### Run 4 — The Crossing

> **Title: What They Did for You**
>
> Alqualonde. You struck first, and your sons followed, because that is
> what sons do. You remember their faces after — not during, never during,
> because during there was only the Oath and the red work — but *after*.
>
> Maedhros, sitting apart, cleaning his sword with hands that would not
> stop shaking. Maglor, who could not sing for three days. Celegorm,
> laughing too loudly, daring anyone to call it wrong. Caranthir, silent,
> which was worse than his raging. Curufin, already calculating the next
> move.
>
> The twins would not look at you.
>
> Then Losgar. The ships burning. You lit them yourself because none of
> your sons would do it — not even Curufin, not even Caranthir. They
> watched the flames, and in the firelight their faces were your father's
> face and your mother's face and Nerdanel's face, and all those faces
> said the same thing: *what have you done?*
>
> But it was done. And your sons stood on the eastern shore of the world
> with you, because the ships were ash and the way back was gone, and the
> Oath was the only road left.

---

#### Run 5 — The Sight

> **Title: Futures Written in Flame**
>
> The dying clarity sharpens. Your sight leaps forward, beyond your own
> death, into the years your sons will live without you.
>
> You see Maedhros on Thangorodrim. Hung by his wrist — the right hand,
> the sword-hand, the hand that first gripped yours in the forge — and
> screaming for death. You see Fingon climb to him. Not one of your sons.
> Fingolfin's son. The boy you betrayed at Losgar. He comes alone, with
> nothing but a harp and a prayer, and he cuts your son free. And
> Maedhros, one-handed, broken, yields the crown you claimed to the house
> you abandoned.
>
> You see Celegorm in Nargothrond, his beauty turned to poison, scheming
> to seize a kingdom that is not his. You see Huan — the hound you
> watched him raise from a pup in Orome's kennels — turn his back and
> walk away, choosing a mortal girl over the master he has loved since
> Valinor.
>
> You see Maedhros raising banners for the Nirnaeth, the great alliance,
> the last hope — and you see the Easterlings turn traitor, and the
> banners fall, and your son retreat to Himring with the light gone from
> his eyes.
>
> These are your sons. This is what the Oath will make of them.

---

#### Run 6 — The Unraveling

> **Title: The Blood You Will Spill Through Them**
>
> Deeper into the vision. Darker.
>
> You see Doriath. The hidden kingdom of Thingol, who wore your father's
> jewel. Your sons — three of them — riding to war against Elves for the
> sake of a Silmaril. Celegorm falls to Dior's sword, golden hair matted
> with blood. Caranthir beside him, dark to the last. And Curufin — your
> mirror, your echo — reaching for the jewel as he dies, and the jewel is
> not there.
>
> Three sons in one day. Kin-slain kin-slayers. The Silmaril escapes
> with a girl and her child.
>
> Then the Havens of Sirion. Refugees. Women. Children. And your two
> youngest sons, the twins who hunted in forests and laughed and could
> not look at you after Losgar, attacking a camp of the dispossessed
> because the Oath demands it and the Oath cannot be refused.
>
> Amrod falls. Amras falls. The youngest flames, the ones that should
> never have been lit, go out among the tents of weeping strangers.
>
> Maglor finds two children in a cave afterward, and takes them in, and
> loves them. And you think: *that is his mother in him. That is
> Nerdanel.* And you are grateful, and you are ashamed, and you are
> dying.

---

#### Run 7 — The Reckoning

> **Title: The Chasm and the Shore**
>
> The last vision. The furthest sight.
>
> Morgoth is overthrown — not by the Noldor, not by the Oath, but by the
> host of the Valar in the War of Wrath, the war your sons could not
> win. The Silmarils lie in the keeping of Eonwe. And your two surviving
> sons — Maedhros and Maglor, the noblest and the gentlest, the ones
> who took most from their mother — steal into the camp of the Valar like
> thieves.
>
> You see Maedhros hold the Silmaril. *Your* Silmaril, the work of your
> hands, the captured light of the Trees. And the jewel — your jewel,
> your masterwork — *burns him*. It does not recognize the hand of
> Feanor's son. Or it recognizes it and finds it stained.
>
> Maedhros walks to the edge of a chasm filled with fire. He looks down.
> He does not hesitate.
>
> You see Maglor on a grey shore, holding the last Silmaril in a hand
> that smokes and blisters, and singing — always singing — and he hurls
> the jewel into the waves and it sinks and the light goes out and
> Maglor walks and walks and walks along the shore, singing, forever,
> a lament that will outlast the world because the world is not long
> enough to contain his grief.
>
> Two sons. The last two. One chose fire. One chose the sea. Neither
> chose home, because there is no home for the Dispossessed.
>
> You see all of this. And the clarity fades, and you are back on the
> ash-field, and their faces — all seven, still alive, still bright,
> still looking to you — swim above you, and you open your mouth.

---

### Vision Chapters (Run Progression — Type 1: Dark/Defeat)

These play when a run ENDS IN DEFEAT (hero dies in Angband). They represent
the vision darkening — Feanor's sight failing, the flame guttering.

---

#### Run 1 Defeat — Ashes in the Cradle

> The memory of their childhood dims. Small faces blur. You cannot
> remember which twin is which. You cannot remember Maglor's first note.
> Maedhros falls from the forge-wall and your hands are not there to
> catch him, and the sound he makes is not laughter. The fire in you
> gutters, and the small flames it kindled gutter with it, and for a
> terrible moment you cannot remember why you had seven sons, only that
> you used them, and they broke.

---

#### Run 2 Defeat — The Mirror Cracks

> Each son's gift twists in the darkening vision. Maedhros's leadership
> becomes stubbornness. Maglor's song becomes a weapon of war. Celegorm's
> wildness becomes cruelty. Caranthir's wrath becomes isolation. Curufin's
> cleverness becomes treachery. The twins' loyalty becomes blindness. You
> made them. You are responsible for every flaw because every flaw is a
> facet of your own flame, and a flawed diamond is still a diamond, and
> still cuts.

---

#### Run 3 Defeat — Seven Chains

> The Oath echoes in the dark, but in this failing vision you hear what
> it sounds like from the *other side* — not the one who speaks but the
> ones who must answer. Seven chains. You forged them yourself, the
> greatest smith in Arda, and each chain is fitted perfectly to the son
> who wears it because you know your sons as only a maker knows his
> works, and you made the chains to hold.

---

#### Run 4 Defeat — The Shore They Cannot Reach

> Losgar burns again, but in this vision you see your sons on the wrong
> side of the fire — not on the eastern shore but trapped on the burning
> ships, reaching across the flames toward a home that recedes with every
> breath. Valinor closes behind them. The Sea turns to stone. And you
> stand on the shore watching, and you cannot tell if you are saving them
> or condemning them, because the fire does not know the difference.

---

#### Run 5 Defeat — The Crumbling Tower

> Himring falls in the darkened vision — not to Morgoth's assault but to
> its own weight, stone pulling from stone, because the foundation was
> laid on oath-cursed ground and nothing cursed can stand forever. Your
> sons' kingdoms crumble one by one, towers sinking into earth, walls
> dissolving, and you realize that everything they built they built on
> *you* — on your pride, your fire, your Oath — and you are dying, and
> the foundation is dying with you.

---

#### Run 6 Defeat — The Face in the Sword

> The Kinslaying at Doriath plays out in nightmare, and in the darkened
> vision every Elf your sons cut down wears your face. Your eyes. Your
> jaw. Your father's brow. Kin killing kin, and the kinship is not
> distant but *intimate* — the Noldor are all one family, and every sword
> your sons swing is aimed at a cousin, an uncle, a mirror. They are
> killing *you*, over and over, because you taught them that the Silmarils
> are worth any blood, and they believed you.

---

#### Run 7 Defeat — The Jewel That Refuses

> The Silmaril burns in Maedhros's hand, and in this last dark vision
> you understand why: not because the Oath is unjust, not because the
> Valar cursed it, but because the jewel *knows its maker*. You made it.
> You hallowed it. You filled it with the light of the Two Trees and
> your own fire. And the fire recognizes what has been done with the Oath
> sworn in its name, and the fire refuses. Your own masterwork judges
> your sons and finds them wanting — finds *you* wanting — and the light
> withdraws, and the burning is the withdrawal, and the chasm is the
> only answer.

---

## V. Hero Victory and Defeat Messages

Each message is written from Feanor's perspective as he gazes at that son's
face and sees the life within it — past and future. Victory means the vision
is bright: the son at his best, his gifts honored. Defeat means the vision
turns to ash: the doom that awaits.

---

### 1. Maedhros

**Victory:**

> *My eldest.* Copper-haired, grave-eyed, bearing his grandfather's name
> as if it were a crown he had not asked for. In this vision Feanor sees
> the Maedhros who will yield the High Kingship to Fingolfin — an act of
> such selfless wisdom that the father cannot comprehend it. To give
> away what is yours? To *choose* diminishment? The Silmaril-light that
> blazes through the vision illuminates a truth Feanor has never
> accepted: that his noblest son is noble *despite* him, not because of
> him. Maedhros's greatness is the part of Nerdanel that survived the
> forge. "You are better than I made you," Feanor whispers. "That is
> your mother's doing. Guard it. The Oath will try to take it from you."

**Defeat:**

> *My eldest.* The vision lurches to the end: Maedhros standing at the
> edge of the chasm, one-handed, holding a Silmaril that burns like
> judgment. His father's jewel does not know him, or knows him and turns
> away. Feanor watches his firstborn step into the fire — the Spirit of
> Fire's eldest son, choosing flame as his father chose flame, but not
> in pride. In despair. And the father sees what the son could not: that
> the chasm is not an ending but an echo, a son repeating the only
> lesson his father ever truly taught — that fire is the answer to
> everything, including fire.

---

### 2. Maglor

**Victory:**

> *My singer.* Your voice was the first beautiful thing you ever made
> that was not metal. Feanor remembers the boy in Tirion, singing scales
> in the garden while the forge-smoke drifted overhead, and how the
> notes cut through the clangor of the hammer like light through cloud.
> In this vision, bright with Silmaril-fire, Maglor is the son who will
> find two orphaned boys in a cave after the ruin of the Havens, and
> lift them up, and love them, and raise them as his own. The kin-slayer
> who becomes a father. The singer whose greatest song is not a lament
> but a lullaby. "You have my fire," Feanor says. "But you turn it to
> warmth instead of burning. That is the one craft I could never master."

**Defeat:**

> *My singer.* The shore stretches without end. Maglor walks it, hand
> ruined, voice carrying across empty water, and the song is beautiful
> and the song is endless and the song will never stop because stopping
> would mean silence and silence would mean facing what he has done.
> Feanor sees the last of his sons — the gentle one, the weeper, the
> singer of lullabies to stolen children — wandering until the world
> is unmade, and he thinks: *I did this.* Not Morgoth. Not the Valar.
> *I* gave him the Oath and the fire to keep it, and the fire burned
> everything except the voice, and the voice cannot stop.

---

### 3. Celegorm

**Victory:**

> *My hunter.* Fair-haired, wild-hearted, faster than thought, laughing
> beneath the trees of Orome's forest with a hound at his side and the
> joy of the chase in every line of his body. In this vision Feanor sees
> the Celegorm who was: the purest expression of the fire's brightness,
> before ambition stained it — a prince who hunted for love of the hunt,
> who ran because running was freedom, who loved his hound above crowns
> and jewels. The Silmaril-light catches Huan's eyes, and the great
> wolfhound gazes back with the steady devotion that Celegorm will one
> day betray. "Of all my sons, you were the most alive," Feanor says.
> "And the Oath will kill that first — the aliveness, the joy — before
> it kills the body."

**Defeat:**

> *My hunter.* Huan turns away. The great hound, companion of Valinor's
> golden days, looks at Celegorm one last time — and the eyes hold no
> anger, only the silence of a beast who loved and can love no longer.
> Feanor watches his fairest son lose the one bond the Oath could not
> command: a hound's simple love, freely given, freely withdrawn. And
> then Doriath, and Celegorm falling to Dior's sword, golden hair in the
> mud, and the Silmaril already gone. "You had everything," Feanor
> whispers. "The beauty, the speed, the hound. And the Oath took each
> one, and last of all it took the hound, and that was the cruelest
> taking, because the hound did not hate you. He only could not follow
> where you chose to go."

---

### 4. Caranthir

**Victory:**

> *My dark one.* The son who took your wrath undiluted, without
> Nerdanel's patience to gentle it. Yet in this bright vision Feanor
> sees what the wrath hid: a builder. Caranthir's wide lands in
> Thargelion, his trade with the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains, the
> wealth he raised not through cunning but through the same stubborn
> force that made his temper terrible. The Silmaril-light shows
> Caranthir at his counting-tables, dark brows furrowed, hands steady —
> not destroying but *making*, in his own fierce way, something that
> might have lasted. "You have my fire, but you pour it into stone,"
> Feanor says. "Into walls and roads and counted treasure. If the Oath
> had not demanded you tear down what you built, your towers might
> still be standing when the sun goes dark."

**Defeat:**

> *My dark one.* The Mereth Aderthad — the Feast of Reuniting, Fingon's
> gift of peace — and Caranthir stands before Angrod and shatters it
> with six words of contempt. The vision darkens. Every bridge his hands
> built, his tongue burned. Every friendship his gold cemented, his wrath
> cracked. He dies at Doriath beside Celegorm and Curufin, and Feanor
> sees his face in the last moment — not enraged but *bewildered*, as
> if the world had finally struck back and he did not understand why.
> "Your wrath was my wrath," Feanor says. "Honest and hot and always
> aimed wrong. I should have taught you where to point it. I was too
> busy pointing my own."

---

### 5. Curufin

**Victory:**

> *My mirror.* Feanor looks at Curufin and sees himself — the same jaw,
> the same eyes, the same hands that know metal before they know speech.
> Of all his sons, only Curufin bears the father-name: Curufinwe,
> "skilled Finwe," as Feanor himself was named. In this vision, bright
> with Silmaril-fire, Feanor sees the craft that justified the name —
> Curufin's hands shaping metal with a precision that approaches
> his own, a smith who might have surpassed even Mahtan had the Oath
> not consumed every hour. But the vision also shows Celebrimbor — the
> grandson, Curufin's son — and his hands are gentler, and his craft is
> purer, and the line of Feanor will produce one more great smith before
> it ends. "You are my face," Feanor says. "But your son will have
> your mother's heart. And that will save what the face could not."

**Defeat:**

> *My mirror.* Celebrimbor stands before his father in Nargothrond and
> speaks: *"I will not follow thee."* And Curufin — Feanor's image,
> Feanor's echo — has nothing to say. The vision darkens. A son disowned
> by his own child: the rejection that even the Oath did not foresee.
> Feanor watches and understands, because he knows the face that
> Celebrimbor is refusing — it is *his* face, reflected through his
> son, and the grandson has seen what the son cannot: that the face of
> Feanor, beautiful and brilliant, is also the face of kin-slaying and
> betrayal, and a child may love his father and still walk away. Curufin
> dies at Doriath reaching for the jewel, and the jewel is not there,
> and the face that lands in the mud is Feanor's own.

---

### 6. Amrod

**Victory:**

> *Little flame.* The younger of the twins — or the elder; even Nerdanel
> could not always tell. Red-haired, quick-footed, trailing after his
> brothers with a bow too large for his small arms. In this vision,
> warm with Silmaril-light, Feanor sees the twins in the forests of
> southern Beleriand, hunting, laughing, moving through the trees as
> if the Oath were a storm on a distant horizon. And for a moment — one
> bright, terrible moment — Feanor allows himself to think: *they could
> have been happy.* Without the Oath, without the Silmarils, without
> him. Two hunters in an endless wood. "You are the proof," Feanor
> whispers, "that the fire I kindled could have been gentle. And I am
> the proof that it was not."

**Defeat:**

> *Little flame.* The ships burn at Losgar, and in the darkening vision
> Feanor cannot look away from the flames and cannot stop counting the
> shapes aboard. Did one of the twins sleep on the ships that night?
> Did his youngest son burn in a fire his father set? The truth has
> never been spoken aloud. The brothers do not ask. The surviving twin
> carries a silence where laughter used to live. And Feanor, dying,
> sees the fire he lit reflected in a child's face — not metaphor, not
> the fire of spirit, but real fire, on real wood, consuming what he
> made and what he loved without distinction, because fire is the one
> craftsman that does not know the difference.

---

### 7. Amras

**Victory:**

> *Little flame.* Mirror of his twin, inseparable, whose loyalty was not
> to the Oath but to his brother, and through his brother to the rest.
> In this bright vision Feanor sees Amras the tracker, the keen-eyed
> one, who could read a trail through water and find north by starlight
> in a land that had never seen the sun. The Silmaril-light falls upon
> the twins together — not warriors, not Feanorians, but *children*,
> two small faces looking up at their father with trust so complete it
> is its own kind of weapon. "You followed because I led," Feanor says.
> "That is the simplest love, and the easiest to betray. I led you
> here. I led you past Alqualonde and over the Sea and into this
> darkened land. And you followed, and you will follow to the end."

**Defeat:**

> *Little flame.* The Havens of Sirion. Smoke and weeping. Refugees
> running, and among the fallen, Amras — the youngest of Feanor's line,
> the hunter-child, the boy who should have spent his ages tracking
> deer in forests that never darken. He dies among people whose only
> crime was having something the Oath demanded. And Feanor sees — in
> the last, the cruelest clarity of death — that the Havens are the
> final answer to the question Nerdanel asked when the twins were born:
> *"What will you make of them?"* This. He made them into this. Two
> hunters with bows too large for their hearts, dying for jewels they
> never wanted, in a war that was over before they were born.

---

## VI. Final Scene — The Command

**This scene ALWAYS plays after the 7th run, regardless of outcomes.**

It consists of three parts: the Knowing, the Choosing, and the Burning.

---

### Part 1: The Knowing

> The visions fade. The futures unseen by any other dying eye dissolve
> back into the ash-grey present. Feanor lies upon the earth beneath
> Thangorodrim's shadow, and his sons — all seven, still alive, still
> whole, still kneeling around him with faces full of grief and love
> and the desperate hope that their father will say something wise,
> something merciful, something that will free them — look down at him
> and wait.
>
> He has seen what they will become. He has seen the Kinslayings — three
> of them, each worse than the last. He has seen Maedhros yield the
> crown and lose his hand and find a chasm. He has seen Celegorm's hound
> walk away. He has seen Curufin disowned by his own child. He has seen
> the twins die among refugees. He has seen Maglor walk the shore
> forever, singing, burning, alone.
>
> He has seen all of it. The clarity is absolute.

---

### Part 2: The Choosing

*This section adjusts based on total campaign score.*

---

#### If total score is HIGH (many silmarils recovered across all runs):

> And he loves them. That is the thing no vision can undo, no clarity
> can burn away. He loves them — the tall one, the singer, the hunter,
> the dark one, the mirror, the twins — loves them as the forge loves
> the metal, fiercely, consumingly, without gentleness.
>
> He opens his mouth and the fire rises in him one last time.
>
> "I have seen what comes. I have seen your roads — every one of them,
> from Thangorodrim to the grey shore. I have seen the crowns you will
> yield and the blood you will spill and the hound that will leave and
> the son who will turn away. I have seen the chasm. I have seen the
> sea. I have seen you fall, and fall, and fall again."
>
> His hand finds Maedhros's hand. The grip is iron.
>
> "And I tell you: *hold the Oath.*"
>
> "Not because you will triumph — you will not. Not because you are
> righteous — you are not, and will be less so with each passing year.
> But because you are *mine*. My sons. My fires. I kindled you in the
> light of the Trees, and the Trees are dead, and the light lives now
> only in the Silmarils, and the Silmarils are stolen, and if you do
> not reclaim them then the last light of Valinor dies in Morgoth's
> iron crown and the darkness wins forever."
>
> "That is what the Oath means. Not vengeance. Not pride. *Light.*
> The preservation of light against the one who would extinguish it.
> This is what I made you for."
>
> "Hold the Oath. Hold it through Kinslaying and ruin and the Doom of
> Mandos itself. Hold it when your hands burn and your friends betray
> you and the world calls you cursed. Hold it because I, your father,
> who see your doom and love you still, command it."
>
> "Seven fires I lit. Let none go dark unspent."

---

#### If total score is LOW (few or no silmarils recovered):

> And he loves them. That is the cruelest part. Not the Oath, not the
> Doom, not Morgoth's malice — but a father's love that will not relent,
> that sees the ruin ahead and refuses to turn aside, that presses the
> sword into the child's hand knowing the child will cut himself.
>
> He opens his mouth and the fire rises, dim now, guttering.
>
> "I have seen what comes. Every road, every end, every price you will
> pay for words I spoke in grief. I have seen the blood. I have seen the
> loneliness. I have seen the moment when the Silmaril burns your hand
> and you understand that the jewel I made does not know my sons."
>
> A pause. The fire almost dies. Then it surges.
>
> "And still I say: *hold the Oath.*"
>
> "What else is there? Valinor is shut. The way back is ash. Your
> grandfather is dead and your mother weeps in a land you will never
> see again. We are the Dispossessed — Mandos named us so, and Mandos
> does not err. We have nothing left but the fire and the words and
> each other."
>
> "Perhaps the words were wrong. Perhaps I was wrong. But the fire is
> real, and the Silmarils are real, and the Enemy who stole them sits
> in iron halls beneath those peaks and *laughs*, and I will not die
> with that laughter unanswered."
>
> "Hold the Oath. I know what it will cost you. I have seen the
> chasm and the shore. I command it anyway, because I am your father,
> and a father does not apologize for the fire he gives his sons, even
> when the fire is ruin."
>
> "Seven fires. Let them burn."

---

### Part 3: The Burning

> His body ignites.
>
> Not from the Balrog-wounds — those are already cold. From *within*.
> The Spirit of Fire, too vast for any vessel of flesh, has spent its
> last fuel and now consumes the vessel itself. The sons stumble back —
> Maedhros pulling the twins, Maglor shielding his eyes, Celegorm's
> hound howling — and the heat is unbearable and beautiful, the last
> light of Feanor blazing white against Thangorodrim's darkness.
>
> When the flames die, there is nothing left.
>
> No body. No bone. No ash that will not scatter. Only a scorch-mark
> on grey earth, already cooling, shaped like a man who burned from
> inside out, and seven sons standing in a circle around the place
> where their father was, and will never be again.
>
> Maedhros speaks first. His voice is steady and his eyes are dry.
> "You heard him. The Oath holds."
>
> Six voices answer. Some steady, some breaking, some barely a whisper.
>
> The Oath holds.
>
> *Seven fires he lit. Not one went dark unspent.*

## VIII. Lore Consistency Notes

### Strict Adherences
- All seven sons' fates follow the Silmarillion exactly
- The birth-order and father-names (Maitimo, Kanafinwe, Tyelkormo, Morifinwe,
  Curufinwe, Pityafinwe, Telufinwe) are from HoME XII
- Nerdanel's tempering influence on the elder sons (Maedhros and Maglor
  inheriting her character) is per HoME XII
- Maedhros's capture, rescue, and yielding of the crown are accurate
- Celegorm's relationship with Huan and Orome is per Silmarillion
- Curufin's renunciation by Celebrimbor in Nargothrond is canon
- The three Kinslayings are in correct chronological order
- The deaths at each Kinslaying are attributed correctly
- Maglor fostering Elrond and Elros is canon
- The theft and burning of the Silmarils is per Silmarillion Ch. 24
- Maedhros's chasm and Maglor's shore are accurate

### Creative Liberties (Tolkien-Compatible)
- Feanor having prophetic dying sight is an expansion of the text — the
  Silmarillion says only that he "knew" the Noldor could not overthrow
  Thangorodrim, but Elven death-sight is a consistent theme in Tolkien (cf.
  Finrod's prophetic knowledge, Galadriel's Mirror, Feanor's own foresight
  about the Silmarils' fate)
- The Amrod/Losgar question is presented as Feanor's own uncertainty, not as
  fact, consistent with the textual ambiguity between published Silmarillion
  and HoME XII
- Feanor's internal justification of the Oath as "preservation of light" is
  an expansion, but consistent with his stated motivations in the Silmarillion
  — he genuinely believed the Silmarils' light must not remain in Morgoth's
  hands
- The emotional interiority of the sons goes beyond Tolkien's deliberately
  terse text but contradicts nothing — the Silmarillion leaves vast space for
  interpretation of the Feanorians' inner lives
